# Zero-shot incident triage: measured pilot and improvement path

The [full evaluation](full-zero-shot-evaluation.md) extends this pilot to all validation, test, and paired challenge records.

This page records **one zero-shot protocol**, not a claim about every checkpoint. The original Jev/Laya/CLM comparison is app run `b80756a81ff248c9` on 2026-09-28, with a [case-level evidence snapshot](../examples/zero-shot-validation20.evidence.json). Kev was evaluated separately in app run `3ed6c46570454f2b`, with its own [case-level evidence snapshot](../examples/kev-zero-shot-validation20.evidence.json). Both runs used byte-identical inputs and labels and identical policy and question-schema hashes. The original local run contains raw responses and probability distributions at `runs/app/b80756a81ff248c9/`; Kev's are at `runs/app/3ed6c46570454f2b/`.

The runs selected the **first 20 validation records**, comprising 10 authored incident families and 20 correlated realizations. Eighteen references say evidence supports an initial domain; two say it does not. This is a pilot, not a random or class-balanced sample. The hashes, requested models, and per-case predictions are in the two evidence files. All five providers used the same public incidents; the four models received the same question schema. All 100 record evaluations completed, including all 80 live model requests. Jev used the moving `jev-latest` alias; the local models were `laya-multilingual@55cf4c4ebb4ebe31b2550e8bdf3bd21b99753851`, `clm-mlx-bf16` with the `czl/CLM-v0.1-8B-MLX@d22299fa619f9bb6fd047de1930517db3bd259a7` encoder and original CLM head (SHA-256 `b2b4a8c9c2d39263eff78a351eb909a342ce9b3bf21a3f07c1d1bf15f1c4eda5`), and `jaredpalmer/kev-4b@139fdd94f1b6a6ad80cc15e08fcb99cac885a101` served through MLX bfloat16 on Apple MPS. Kev source revision was `3e1cd3bb588a388a06827443380befece23e68c7`; its base was `Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B-Base@1001bb4d826a52d1f399e183466143f4da7b741b`.

“All four correct” is joint correctness on the same incident. Kev's 8/20 is below Jev's 11/20 but far above the two other local models' 0/20 under this protocol. Kev got owner and next check both correct on 10/20 cases, versus 16/20 for Jev, 1/20 for Laya, and 0/20 for CLM. Kev's seven extra correct priorities relative to Jev did not close its owner/check and evidence-flag gaps. On paired cases, Jev alone got six incidents fully correct, Kev alone got three, both got five, and neither got six. The sample is too small and family-correlated to establish a general model ranking. Kev is zero-shot only with respect to **Northstar**: its released checkpoint was trained on other decision data, with no Northstar training or prompt tuning in this run.

Kev's owner errors were five supported domains routed to `noc` (three radio, two power). For next check, it chose `verify_change` on three transport cases and `gather_evidence` on seven supported-domain cases. It found both reference `insufficient_evidence=yes` cases, but also flagged six of the 18 `no` cases. These are concrete candidates for controlled prompt or training experiments; the two positive cases are too few to tune a trustworthy threshold. Its successful-call median latency was 4.70 seconds and p95 was 8.96 seconds on this Mac, with a 109-second serial run; these are deployment-specific measurements, not a throughput benchmark.

Field accuracy shows that Laya and CLM's 0% is not merely a harsh joint metric. CLM selected **transport / P2 / gather_evidence / yes on all 20 cases**. Laya selected only `noc` or `transport` for owner and answered `yes` to insufficient evidence on all 20. Both therefore recognized just the two reference `yes` cases. CLM's 20 request hashes and owner distributions differ, and 15 calls consumed fresh encoder tokens; this is not a replayed response or a failed transport. Its top choice stayed constant while the probabilities changed. The `yes` probabilities were high: median 0.915 for CLM and 0.959 for Laya. These numbers are model scores, not validated real-world confidence.

As an **offline diagnostic, not a new model result**, substituting the exact policy rule for priority changed joint correctness to 16/20 Jev, 1/20 Laya, and 0/20 CLM. Deriving the evidence flag from the predicted owner/check did not raise those last two counts. Deterministic policy code is valuable for a hybrid system, but it cannot compensate for wrong domain and diagnostic decisions here. This diagnostic predates the Kev supplement; no Kev hybrid score is implied.

The simple MLX wiring explanation has less support than the raw collapse suggests. The live CLM service returned distinct case distributions, and two separate canaries were close to the conversion maintainer's published bf16 MLX reference: invoice department `billing=0.98769` here versus `0.98818` in [the model card](https://huggingface.co/czl/CLM-v0.1-8B-MLX), and tides ranking `0.99329` versus `0.99386`. This does **not** establish numerical parity with the upstream vLLM deployment or across all inputs. Laya's [package documentation](https://github.com/NandhaKishorM/laya) explicitly reports near-chance zero-shot typed-decision accuracy for its base checkpoints, with much better results after task-specific fine-tuning on a different dataset. Those published numbers are context, not Northstar results.

## How to improve and test it

1. **Preserve these zero-shot results.** Keep the current protocol and both evidence snapshots unchanged. Replicate on all 220 validation records and report family-grouped intervals, confusion matrices, probability reliability, and failures. The 20 records include only two positive evidence cases, so a threshold or polarity change selected on them would overfit. Before changing any prompt, have a network specialist audit representative labels and the fictional policy. Synthetic prose and repeated realizations can exaggerate apparent certainty.
2. **Validate serving equivalence.** Pin Jev to a supported version if possible, retain the exact Laya and CLM revisions and CLM head hash, and check more reference canaries. For CLM, compare the same text embeddings and decisions with the upstream Qwen3/vLLM pooling path on a suitable GPU before claiming Mac/reference equivalence. Keep adapter failures separate from model errors. The two canaries above are reassuring, not a full parity test.
3. **Run controlled input and question ablations on validation only.** Try a compact, decision-focused incident rendering that preserves every decision-relevant public fact; remove duplicated ticket prose and irrelevant topology detail only when it does not affect the answer. Try shorter, affirmative field questions and fixed, action-oriented candidate descriptions. CLM embeds candidate descriptions verbatim; Laya includes option keys in its rendered choices, so one JSON schema is not identical model input. Compare one change at a time against the frozen zero-shot protocol. Keep each variant's exact prompt, hashes, model revision, latency, and per-case predictions. Small local probes so far show wording sensitivity but have **not** established an accuracy gain.
4. **Specialize on training data.** Use the 600 training realizations from 30 families, never validation/test labels, for model-specific training adapters. For Laya, start with a small domain-specific decision head, then compare with broader encoder/head fine-tuning using its published training workflow. For CLM, keep the Qwen3 encoder fixed initially, adapt the original state/action projection heads with matched state–candidate pairs and hard negatives (especially `noc` versus specific domains and `gather_evidence` versus a domain check). The repository's `export-training` output is model-neutral and requires conversion to each trainer's expected format. Treat any specialized checkpoint as a **trained** model, separately from the zero-shot finding. Thirty independent training families may be too few; collect additional independently authored, specialist-reviewed families if validation remains brittle.
5. **Use deterministic policy and selective escalation as a separate system track.** Priority follows explicit structured impact rules and should normally be computed in code. A calibrated abstention threshold can route uncertain owner/check cases to an engineer or Jev; choose it on validation and report both coverage and error. Do not claim improved model accuracy from replacing its outputs with rules. In this pilot, the evidence scores do not support a simple 0.5 decision threshold, and there are too few positive cases to fit a trustworthy alternative.
6. **Freeze a candidate and evaluate once on held-out data.** Compare unmodified Jev, zero-shot local models including Kev, prompt-adapted models, trained models, and hybrids on the same final protocol and incidents. Report joint and field accuracy, severe-priority misses, confidence calibration, family-grouped uncertainty, latency, and cost. The full test and paired challenge sets must stay out of prompt selection, training, and calibration. Matching Jev's 11/20 pilot score alone is not sufficient when the rules baseline reached 19/20; the real goal is reliable decisions on independent cases.

The most informative early milestone is **owner plus next-check correctness**. Kev reached 10/20 on that pair, which is progress over CLM's 0/20 and Laya's 1/20 but below Jev's 16/20. A better priority rule or evidence threshold cannot fix the remaining owner/check errors. Require a measured gain on those two fields before spending time on calibration or production-style automation.
